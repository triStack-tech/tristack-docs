A domain has to be verified before anything can be sent from it, and a verified domain belongs
to exactly one project.

## The statuses

| Status | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `Pending` | Added, DNS not yet confirmed. Rechecked automatically every few minutes. |
| `Verified` | Usable. Any address at this domain can be a `from`. |
| `Failed` | Something went wrong that rechecking will not fix. `error` says what. |
| `Expired` | Not verified within seven days. Released, and can be added again. |

You never poll for verification and you never trigger a recheck. Tristack does it.

## One domain, one project

A domain can be claimed by one project and no other, across every account on Tristack. A second
claim is refused with `domain_taken`.

That response deliberately does not say who holds it. Telling you would turn this endpoint into
a way of discovering which companies are Tristack customers.

If you hold the domain on another of your own projects, remove it there first and it becomes
available immediately.

## Limits

A project may hold **25 domains** in any state, `Pending` included. The cap counts claims, not
verifications, so abandoned attempts count against you until they expire or you remove them.

## When verification will not complete

Almost every stuck domain is one of these.

**The record name got doubled.** Most DNS providers append the zone automatically. Saving a
name of `abc._domainkey.yourdomain.com` in such a provider produces
`abc._domainkey.yourdomain.com.yourdomain.com`, which resolves to nothing. Look at the saved
record, not what you typed. If the provider shows the full name, enter only the part before
your domain.

**Only some of the records were added.** All three are required. One missing record means the
domain never verifies, and nothing will tell you which one.

**The records were added to the wrong zone.** A domain whose nameservers point at one provider
while you edit the zone at another will never verify. Check where the nameservers actually
point before editing anything.

**A CAA or CNAME flattening feature rewrote them.** Some providers flatten CNAMEs at the apex
or proxy them. These records must be plain, unproxied CNAMEs.

Verify what is actually published rather than what you believe you saved:

```bash
dig +short r73jcizhhvz5grb5cfuxusw7yogsnwd5._domainkey.yourdomain.com CNAME
```

It should print the matching `....dkim.amazonses.com` value. If it prints nothing, DNS is the
problem and Tristack cannot see the record either.

## Removing a domain

Removing a domain releases the name for anyone to claim and stops sending from it immediately.
Mail already accepted is unaffected: it has been handed to the provider and will be delivered.

```bash
curl -X DELETE https://api.tristack.tech/v1/relay/email/domains/yourdomain.com \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TRISTACK_API_KEY"
```

## Check now

A pending domain has a **Check now** button. It does not make verification happen: that runs
on its own every few minutes either way. What it does is tell you **which record is wrong**,
which the automatic check cannot.

It resolves each of the three CNAMEs in public DNS and reports one of four things per record:

| Verdict | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| OK | Published, pointing where it should. |
| Not published | Nothing resolves at that name. |
| Wrong value | Something resolves, but not the value we issued. It shows you what it found. |
| Wrong name | Found one zone deeper. Your provider appended the domain to a name that already ended with it. |

That last one is the common failure, and it is invisible without this check: the correct name
simply resolves to nothing, so it looks identical to having published nothing at all.

Limited to one check per domain every 30 seconds. Checking a different domain is not affected.

## A second DMARC record breaks DMARC

If `_dmarc.yourdomain.com` returns **more than one** TXT record starting `v=DMARC1`, receivers
treat your domain as having **no DMARC policy at all** and ignore every one of them. It is the
same trap as publishing two SPF records.

This is easy to do by accident: some DNS providers publish a DMARC record for you through a
managed feature, and adding your own by hand leaves two. Check what is actually published
rather than what you think you added:

```bash
dig +short _dmarc.yourdomain.com TXT
```

More than one line back means DMARC is not being applied. Delete until one remains. If one of
them collects reports for you, keep that one.

## Optional records

Alongside the three required CNAMEs, the domain screen carries an **Optional records** section.
Nothing in it is needed to verify or to send, and each entry says what it is for:

**DMARC**, a TXT record at `_dmarc.yourdomain.com`. Tells receiving servers what to do with
mail claiming to be from you that fails authentication, and asks them to send you reports.
Gmail and Yahoo have required it from bulk senders since 2024, so its absence matters more as
your volume grows.

**A return path on your own domain**, an MX and a TXT record on `mail.yourdomain.com`. Publish
both or neither.

They give you two things. Bounce notifications reach a domain you control rather than the
sending platform's. And more usefully, they add **SPF alignment alongside DKIM alignment**, so
DMARC has two independent ways to pass. Without them DMARC rests on DKIM alone, which means a
key rotation problem, or a forwarding hop that breaks the signature, takes DMARC down with it.

<Note>
  You may read that a mismatched return path makes Gmail annotate your mail as sent "via"
  another domain. That happens only when there is no aligned DKIM signature. Your mail is
  DKIM-signed with a key on your own domain, so the signature aligns and no annotation appears.
  Verified against a real delivery, not assumed.
</Note>

<Note>
  You will see advice elsewhere to add an SPF record to your domain itself. With the default
  return path that does nothing: SPF is evaluated against the return-path domain, which is not
  yours, so your record is never consulted. DMARC passes on DKIM alignment alone. SPF becomes
  meaningful only alongside the MX record above, which is why it is offered there and not on
  its own.
</Note>
